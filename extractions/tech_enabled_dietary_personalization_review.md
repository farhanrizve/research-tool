**Title:** Artificial Intelligence and Nutritionist-Guided Dietary
Personalization: A Systematic Review of Clinical and Technological
Integration

**Abstract**: This comprehensive review examines the ways in which
Artificial Intelligence (AI) and nutritionist-led approaches are
transforming individualized nutrition, analyzing their distinct effects
on eating patterns and client compliance. Using PRISMA methodology,
research published from 2016 to 2024 was systematically analyzed across
six primary databases to assess how each system provides targeted
dietary guidance. AI-based platforms combine multi-omics, behavioral,
and clinical information to enhance nutritional strategies, whereas
nutritionist-directed programs focus on behavioral coaching and cultural
adaptation.

Throughout various health conditions---such as diabetes, heart disease,
chronic kidney disease, and microbiome-associated disorders---AI
technologies showed excellent capabilities in data analysis, biomarker
forecasting, and expansion potential. Conversely,
nutritionist-supervised programs consistently produced superior
long-term compliance, situational appropriateness, and client
contentment. These results collectively demonstrate the synergistic
possibilities of AI and human knowledge in developing clinically
meaningful, culturally appropriate individualized nutrition.
Nevertheless, existing research faces limitations from insufficient
validation trials, variable reporting standards, and brief study
periods. The analysis determines that combined AI-nutritionist
approaches, reinforced by thorough validation and responsible data
management, could merge precision with personalization for practical
dietary healthcare applications.

**Keywords:** Artificial Intelligence, Personalized Nutrition, Dietary
Recommendations, Hybrid Models, Precision Health

1.  **Introduction**

The nutrition sector is experiencing a major shift with the introduction
of artificial intelligence (AI) (Pradhan N, et al.; Han K, et al.; King
A, et al.). Traditional dietary guidelines often offer broad advice that
fails to cater sufficiently to individual needs and preferences
(Shamanna P, et al.; Rein M, et al.; Sajid M, et al.). AI has the
potential to transform nutrition by developing personalized dietary
plans that consider a wide range of factors such as medical history,
genetic tendencies, lifestyle habits, and food preferences (Dellino M,
et al.; Naidu AS, et al.; Pelton SR, et al.). The shift from
conventional nutritional care to tele-nutrition, which was hastened by
the COVID-19 pandemic, has spurred the development of AI-based tools
(Heithoff DM, et al.; Rouskas K, et al.). These tools can support health
care providers in delivering remote dietary evaluations and tailored
recommendations (Pradhan N, et al.; King A, et al.; Pérez-Beltrán YE, et
al.). AI-powered systems can analyze extensive datasets to uncover
patterns and connections between dietary habits and health results, thus
facilitating the development of highly personalized nutrition plans
(Rivera-Íñiguez I, et al.; Gutiérrez-Esparza G, et al.; Kassem H, et
al.).

Despite these promising advances, several critical gaps remain in the
field. Firstly, there is a lack of comprehensive integration between
AI-driven algorithms and human nutritionist expertise, which limits the
practical applicability and clinical adoption of AI-based dietary
recommendations (Pradhan N, et al.; King A, et al.; Gutiérrez-Esparza G,
et al.). Secondly, many existing models inadequately address cultural
relevance and ethical considerations, such as data privacy, algorithmic
bias, and patient autonomy, which are essential for equitable and
effective personalized nutrition across diverse populations (Pelton SR,
et al.; Gutiérrez-Esparza G, et al.). Thirdly, evidence regarding
long-term patient adherence and real-world health outcomes of
AI-personalized diets remains limited (Rein M, et al.; Sajid M, et al.;
Pérez-Beltrán YE, et al.; Kassem H, et al.).

This systematic review aims to address these gaps by investigating the
current role of AI in personalized nutrition, specifically examining how
AI and nutritionist-guided approaches compare and complement each other
(Pradhan N, et al.; Gutiérrez-Esparza G, et al.; Kassem H, et al.). The
review evaluates the applications, advantages, and challenges of AI in
delivering culturally relevant, clinically validated, and
patient-centered dietary guidance (King A, et al.; Naidu AS, et al.;
Pérez-Beltrán YE, et al.). The overarching research question guiding
this work is: How effectively can artificial intelligence integrate with
human expertise to provide personalized, culturally sensitive, and
clinically impactful nutrition recommendations, and what are the
critical barriers to its widespread implementation (Pradhan N, et al.;
Gutiérrez-Esparza G, et al.; Kassem H, et al.)? Through a comprehensive
review of recent literature, this study seeks to illuminate pathways
toward optimized personalized nutrition that synthesizes technological
innovation with human judgment (Pradhan N, et al.; Han K, et al.; Kassem
H, et al.).

###  **Methodology**

This comprehensive review investigates the implementation of Artificial
Intelligence (AI) and nutrition specialist-directed approaches in
customized dietary planning, adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items
for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework (Elkhwesky
Z, Salem IE, Ramkissoon H, Castañeda-García JA). The methodology was
structured to guarantee transparency, replicability, and thoroughness
when locating and assessing pertinent research studies.

**2.1 Search Strategy and Data Sources**

A systematic search was conducted across six electronic databases---Web
of Science (WoS), PubMed, IEEE Xplore, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Google
Scholar---covering publications from January 2016 to June 2024. This
timeframe was selected to capture both pre-pandemic and post-pandemic
developments in AI applications to nutrition.

**2.3 Data Extraction and Synthesis**

For each eligible study, data were extracted systematically using a
structured data extraction sheet, capturing the following elements:

- Study design and country of origin

- Intervention type (AI-driven or nutritionist-guided)

- Participant characteristics and sample size

- Data sources (e.g., clinical, genomic, microbiome, behavioral)

- Main outcomes (clinical, metabolic, or adherence measures)

- Limitations and implications

Quantitative results were summarized narratively, given the
heterogeneity of study designs and outcome measures. Comparative themes
were synthesized across diseases (e.g., diabetes, CVD, CKD, microbiome)
to identify cross-cutting trends.

- **Selection Bias:** Mitigated by predefined eligibility criteria and
  dual independent screening.

- **Language Bias:** Acknowledged, as only English studies were

- **Reporting Bias:** Minimized through adherence to PRISMA and
  inclusion of both positive and negative findings.

This study utilizes a systematic review methodology to investigate the
role of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in personalized nutrition. The
review was conducted in line with the PRISMA framework, covering the
four standard stages---Identification, Screening, Eligibility, and
Inclusion---to ensure rigor, transparency, and reproducibility

To ensure broad coverage, articles were retrieved from multiple credible
databases, including Web of Science (WoS), PubMed, IEEE Xplore, Google
Scholar, Scopus, and ScienceDirect. This diverse selection provided
access to biomedical, computational, and interdisciplinary studies as
well as grey literature.

An initial 112 articles were identified. After removing duplicates and
screening for relevance based on titles and abstracts, 45 studies were
retained for full-text review. Following the application of eligibility
criteria, a total of 30 articles were included in the final analysis.
These studies were assessed for their application of AI/ML techniques in
nutrition, integration of personalization aspects such as genomics,
metabolomics, and microbiome data, and their reporting of clinical,
metabolic, or behavioral health outcomes.

As shown in Figure 2, the distribution of methodologies among the 30
included articles is as follows:

- Quantitative studies (40%): 12 articles used numerical datasets,
  randomized trials, or computational models to assess AI's performance
  in nutrition personalization.

- Qualitative studies (13.3%): 4 articles explored patient experiences,
  professional perspectives, or conceptual debates about AI-guided
  nutrition.

- Mixed-methods studies (20%): 6 articles integrated both quantitative
  analysis and qualitative insights, providing a holistic perspective.

- Conceptual or narrative studies (26.7%): 8 articles presented
  theoretical frameworks, literature syntheses, or models of AI
  applications in nutrition without empirical validation.

This methodological mix ensures a balanced perspective, combining
statistical evidence with theoretical depth and experiential insights,
thereby enriching the critical analysis of AI's role in precision
nutrition.

**3. Discussion and Comparative Analysis**

**3.1 Integrating Evidence Across Diseases**

The reviewed body of evidence reveals that AI-driven systems and
nutritionist-guided approaches represent two complementary but distinct
paradigms in personalized nutrition.

AI models excel in data integration, predictive analytics, and
scalability, whereas human nutritionists contribute uniquely through
contextual interpretation, behavioral counseling, and cultural
adaptation.

Across multiple chronic diseases---diabetes, cardiovascular disease
(CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and gut-microbiome-related
disorders---AI systems consistently outperform traditional methods in
generating rapid, data-rich, and precision-based dietary
recommendations. Machine-learning algorithms effectively link clinical,
genomic, and lifestyle variables with outcome prediction, improving
metabolic markers such as glycemic control and triglyceride reduction.

However, long-term adherence and psychosocial engagement remain domains
where human-led nutrition care shows stronger outcomes, indicating that
effective personalization extends beyond algorithmic optimization.

**3.2 Disease-Specific Insights and Cross-Domain Themes**

**3.2.1 Critical Analysis for Personalized Nutrition in Diabetes:**

"Personalized Nutrition in Type 2 Diabetes Remission: Application of
Digital Twin Technology for Predictive Glycemic Control" aimed to
develop and apply a Digital Twin (DT) technology---a machine learning
platform integrating continuous glucose monitoring, dietary,
anthropometric, and gut microbiome data---to predict and modulate
postprandial glycemic responses (PPGRs) in type 2 diabetes (T2D)
patients, thereby improving glycemic control and supporting
remission.Preliminary randomized controlled trial (RCT) and real-world
data showed that the DT-driven personalized nutrition plan significantly
reduced HbA1c, improved beta-cell function, normalized hyperinsulinemia,
and improved markers of metabolic-associated fatty liver disease
(MAFLD), blood pressure, and cardiovascular risk factors. The approach
addressed individual variability in PPGRs using advanced machine
learning models like CatBoostRegressor and Random Forest. The study
highlighted the importance of modifiable factors such as gut microbiome
composition and meal timing in glycemic response variability.The model's
reliance on complex features like CGM and fecal analyses may limit broad
applicability. The study focused mainly on T2D patients, and some health
biomarkers were self-reported or remotely collected, introducing
potential measurement errors. The approach requires further validation
in larger cohorts and diverse populations (Shamanna P, Joshi S,
Thajudeen M, Shah L, Poon T, Mohamed M, et al).

"Effects of Personalized Diets by Prediction of Glycemic Responses on
Glycemic Control and Metabolic Health in Newly Diagnosed T2DM: A
Randomized Dietary Intervention Pilot Trial" To evaluate the clinical
effects of a personalized postprandial-targeting (PPT) diet, based on
machine learning predictions of individual PPGRs incorporating clinical
and microbiome data, compared to a Mediterranean-style diet in newly
diagnosed T2D patients. In a randomized crossover trial with 23 adults,
the PPT diet significantly lowered continuous glucose monitoring
(CGM)-based measures including average PPGR, mean glucose, and time
spent with glucose \>140 mg/dL compared to the Mediterranean diet. A
subsequent 6-month PPT intervention in 16 participants led to
significant improvements in HbA1c, fasting glucose, triglycerides, and
achieved diabetes remission in 61% of participants. Improvements
correlated with individual gut microbiome changes. The small sample size
and short duration of the initial crossover phase limit
generalizability. The study population was newly diagnosed T2D patients,
which may not represent those with long-standing diabetes or other
comorbidities. Longer and larger studies are needed to confirm sustained
benefits (Rein M, Ben-Yacov O,Godneva A, Shilo S, Zmora N, Kolobkov D,
et al).

Next-generation diabetes diagnosis and personalized diet-activity
management: A hybrid ensemble paradigm aims to develop a hybrid machine
learning framework for early and accurate prediction of diabetes and to
provide personalized diet and exercise recommendations, thereby
improving diabetes management and reducing reliance on frequent medical
visits and costly medications. The authors employ a combination of
advanced machine learning classifiers---Random Forest, Support Vector
Machine, and XGBoost---to construct a robust model for diabetes
prediction. The framework also features a comprehensive food and
exercise recommender system, which generates individualized and
health-conscious nutritional guidance based on user preferences and
medical profiles. The system leverages a large dataset, comprising over
100 million user-rated foods, to enhance the accuracy and relevance of
its recommendations. The hybrid model demonstrates a prediction error
rate of less than 30%, indicating reasonable accuracy in diabetes risk
assessment. The integrated recommender system offers tailored dietary
and exercise plans, which have the potential to support better glycemic
control and reduce the economic burden associated with diabetes care.
The study highlights the potential of combining machine learning with
personalized nutrition to facilitate early detection and proactive
management of diabetes.While the model achieves promising results, its
performance is potentially constrained by the quality and quantity of
labeled data, as well as the presence of outliers in diabetes datasets.
Additionally, the study does not extensively address the practical
aspects of implementing such a system in real-world settings, including
user adherence to recommendations and integration with existing
healthcare infrastructure (Sajid M, Malik KR, Khan AH, Iqbal S,
Alaulamie AA, Ilyas QM).

The reviewed articles collectively underscore the growing potential of
personalized nutrition driven by machine learning and biological data
integration in managing and potentially reversing type 2 diabetes (T2D).
The study titled "Personalized Nutrition in Type 2 Diabetes Remission:
Application of Digital Twin Technology for Predictive Glycemic Control"
showcases a cutting-edge approach using Digital Twin (DT) technology
that combines CGM, dietary habits, anthropometric data, and gut
microbiome profiles to tailor nutritional recommendations. The machine
learning models (e.g., CatBoostRegressor, Random Forest) effectively
addressed inter-individual variability in postprandial glycemic
responses (PPGRs), demonstrating meaningful improvements in HbA1c,
insulin sensitivity, and cardiovascular risk markers. However, the
complexity of the model---requiring CGM and microbiome analysis---poses
practical limitations in scalability and accessibility, particularly in
low-resource settings. Moreover, reliance on self-reported data
introduces potential measurement biases. Similarly, the second study,
"Effects of Personalized Diets by Prediction of Glycemic Responses on
Glycemic Control and Metabolic Health in Newly Diagnosed T2DM", presents
compelling evidence from a randomized crossover trial showing that a PPT
diet based on machine learning predictions significantly improved
glycemic metrics compared to a traditional Mediterranean diet. Notably,
61% of participants achieved diabetes remission during a 6-month
follow-up, with benefits linked to gut microbiome changes. Despite these
promising results, the small sample size and short trial duration limit
the generalizability and long-term applicability of the findings. In
contrast, the third study, "Next-generation diabetes diagnosis and
personalized diet-activity management: A hybrid ensemble paradigm,"
focuses more on early prediction of diabetes risk and broad management
strategies, incorporating diet and exercise recommendations through a
machine learning-based recommender system. By using a hybrid ensemble of
classifiers (Random Forest, SVM, XGBoost) and a massive dietary dataset,
the study demonstrates that AI can aid in proactive risk assessment and
lifestyle modification. However, its predictive accuracy (error rate
\<30%) is moderate, and real-world implementation concerns---such as
user engagement, recommendation adherence, and system integration into
clinical workflows---remain underexplored.

Both AI-driven and nutritionist-guided personalized nutrition approaches
demonstrate promising outcomes in diabetes management, yet they differ
in important ways. AI approaches, such as digital twin technology and
hybrid machine learning models, offer scalability, high-resolution data
integration (e.g., continuous glucose monitoring, microbiome profiles),
and predictive precision, enabling customized glycemic control and early
diabetes risk identification. However, these systems often entail
considerable costs due to complex data requirements and specialized
devices, and their applicability in routine clinical settings remains
limited by technological accessibility and patient digital literacy.
Conversely, nutritionist-guided interventions leverage expert clinical
judgment and patient-centered counseling, promoting tailored dietary
plans with consideration of cultural preferences and behavioral factors,
which can enhance patient adherence. Despite potentially lower
scalability, these approaches benefit from established trust and
real-world usability. Challenges common to both include limited
long-term adherence data and the need to validate sustained health
outcomes in diverse populations. Integrating AI tools with clinician
guidance may optimize diabetes care by combining precision with
personalized support.

**3.2.2 Personalized Dietary Recommendations Based on Lipid-Related
Genetic Variants**

"Personalized Dietary Recommendations Based on Lipid-Related Genetic
Variants" aimed to identify nutrigenetic recommendations demonstrating
strong gene-diet interactions affecting circulating lipid levels. Using
PRISMA guidelines, 38 studies (2010--2020) from PubMed and ScienceDirect
were analyzed, focusing on gene-diet interactions with macronutrients,
supplements, and dietary components. Specific dietary strategies, such
as adjusting polyunsaturated (PUFA) and monounsaturated (MUFA) fatty
acid intake, nutraceuticals (e.g., omega-3), and dietary fiber, were
linked to improved lipid profiles in individuals with genetic variants
like APOE and LPL. Exclusion of non-English studies, potential
publication bias, and heterogeneity in study designs limited
generalizability (Pérez-Beltrán YE, Rivera-Iñiguez I, Gonzalez-Becerra
K, Pérez-Naitoh N, Tovar J, Sáyago-Ayerdi SG, et al).

"Lipid-Related Genetic Variants for Personalized Dietary Interventions"
sought to define a panel of genetic variants associated with
dyslipidemia for precision nutrition interventions. Screening 3,031
articles via PRISMA-P, 51 studies met inclusion criteria, focusing on
genes involved in lipid synthesis, metabolism, and clearance. Variants
in FABP2, LPL, CETP, and APOE were strongly associated with lipid
abnormalities, suggesting tailored dietary strategies (e.g., MUFA-rich
diets for CETP rs5882 carriers)\[. Narrow focus on specific genes,
limited ethnic diversity, and insufficient functional validation of
variants were noted (Rivera-Íñiguez I, González-Becerra K, Ramos-Lopez
O, Pérez-Beltrán YE, Chagüén-Hernández MS, Martínez-López E, et al).

\"A machine learning approach to personalized predictors of
dyslipidemia: a cohort study\" aimed to identify the most relevant
factors associated with different types of dyslipidemia---namely
hypertriglyceridemia, hypercholesterolemia, hypoalphalipoproteinemia,
and mixed hyperlipidemias---using data from the Tlalpan 2020 cohort in
Mexico City. By applying machine learning techniques such as Random
Forest (RF), XGBoost, and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), and using
SMOTE to address class imbalance, the study analyzed 2,621 adults aged
20 to 50 across a diverse set of variables, including anthropometric,
biochemical, dietary, familial, and psychological factors. Separate
models were trained and evaluated for men and women to capture
gender-specific predictors. Results showed that the RF-based Variable
Importance Measures (VIM) yielded the most effective feature subsets,
achieving balanced accuracy rates of up to 83.69% for men with
hypercholesterolemia and 83.65% for women with hypoalphalipoproteinemia.
Key risk factors identified included BMI, uric acid levels, age, sleep
disturbances, and anxiety, though the importance of each varied by
gender and dyslipidemia type. Despite its strong predictive performance,
the study's limitations include its cross-sectional design---preventing
causal inference---and the lack of external validation, which may limit
generalizability. Furthermore, some novel predictors identified need
further exploration to clarify their biological relevance and clinical
utility (Gutiérrez-Esparza G, et al).

The three reviewed articles collectively underscore the growing
importance of integrating genetics and advanced data analytics for
personalized management of dyslipidemia, yet they also expose
significant methodological and translational limitations. The first two
systematic reviews demonstrate that gene-diet
interactions---particularly involving variants in APOE, LPL, CETP, and
FABP2---can meaningfully inform targeted dietary interventions, such as
adjusting PUFA/MUFA intake or utilizing nutraceuticals like omega-3s.
These findings suggest actionable routes toward precision nutrition, but
both reviews are hampered by issues that limit their applicability:
language and publication bias, a narrow gene-centric focus, limited
ethnic diversity among study populations, and insufficient functional
validation of implicated variants. Moreover, the heterogeneity of
nutritional exposures and study designs complicates synthesis and
practical translation. The third article broadens the risk assessment
for dyslipidemia beyond genetics, implementing robust machine learning
models to evaluate phenotypic, behavioral, and environmental factors
within a Mexican cohort. Its gender-stratified, high-accuracy models
highlight modifiable risk factors (e.g., BMI, uric acid, sleep,
anxiety), demonstrating the potential of machine learning for nuanced,
population-specific risk stratification. However, this study's
cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and its lack of
external validation raises questions about generalizability. Notably,
all three articles fall short in bridging genotypic findings directly to
comprehensive clinical recommendations---either through limited gene
panels, uncertainty about biological mechanisms, or omission of
multi-omic integration. Future research would benefit from longitudinal
designs, diverse cohorts, deeper functional investigation of variants,
and, crucially, combining genetic, phenotypic, and lifestyle factors to
develop validated, actionable, and equitable frameworks for dyslipidemia
prevention and management.

**3.2.3 Critical Analysis for Personalized Dietary in Heart Objectives**

The study "Artificial Intelligence-Generated Diet Plans for Hypertension
and Dyslipidemia" aimed to assess the quality and adherence of
ChatGPT-generated diet plans for simulated patients with hypertension
and dyslipidemia, comparing the AI's output with DASH and Mediterranean
diet guidelines. Using ChatGPT-4o, dietary plans were created for 24
simulated individuals (age 56; BMI-based categories) with hypertension
and elevated LDL levels. Nutrient content and adherence to dietary
guidelines were assessed using validated scoring systems. Despite
reasonable recommendations like weight loss, omega-3 intake, and
physical activity, the AI-generated plans showed low adherence to DASH
(scores \<4.5) and Mediterranean diet models (scores \<9). Nutrient
profiles lacked significant differentiation by condition, and no advice
was provided regarding smoking or alcohol use. The AI prompts used were
basic and did not request personalization beyond health conditions. No
cultural preferences or behavioral considerations were included,
limiting real-world applicability (Kenger EB, Karahan TÖ).

The study "A Clinical Trial of the Effects of a Dietary Pattern on
Health Metrics and Fecal Metabolites in Volunteers With Risk of
Cardiovascular Disease" objective was to evaluate the effects of the
Guangxi longevity dietary pattern on cardiovascular risk factors and gut
metabolite profiles in individuals at high risk for cardiovascular
disease (CVD). Fourteen volunteers underwent a 2-week dietary
intervention using a traditional Guangxi longevity diet, with pre- and
post-intervention assessments of anthropometric data, blood lipids, and
fecal metabolomics. There were significant reductions in body weight,
BMI, LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, and blood pressure. HDL
cholesterol increased, and fecal metabolomics revealed shifts in key
metabolites related to cardiovascular and metabolic health. The study
had a small sample size, short duration, and lacked a control group. The
findings, while promising, are not generalizable without larger,
randomized studies (Han K, et al).

The study "An Interpretable Machine Learning Model with Demographic
Variables and Dietary Patterns for ASCVD Identification"aimed to build
an interpretable machine learning (ML) model linking dietary and
demographic factors to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD)
risk. The study analyzed NHANES data (1999--2018) from 40,298
participants using five ML models. XGBoost emerged as the most effective
in predicting ASCVD with an AUC of 0.8143. Male gender, older age,
smoking, and lower dairy intake were associated with higher ASCVD risk.
Unexpectedly, reduced refined grain intake did not lower ASCVD risk.
Dairy intake had a protective correlation. Being observational,
causality cannot be inferred. Also, the study didn't test dietary
interventions directly, nor did it offer personalized diet plans (Tang
Q, Wang Y, Luo Y).

The study 'Effects of a Personalized Nutrition Program on
Cardiometabolic Health: A Randomized Controlled Trial" aimed to assess
the efficacy of an 18-week personalized dietary program (PDP) on
cardiometabolic outcomes compared to general dietary advice. A total of
347 participants were randomized to either a PDP group, which used
glycemic and lipid response data, microbiome profiling, and personal
health history, or a control group receiving standard USDA dietary
guidance. The primary outcomes measured were LDL-C and triglyceride
levels. The PDP group showed a significant reduction in triglycerides
(−0.13 mmol/L; p=0.016), though the change in LDL-C was not
statistically significant. Secondary benefits observed in the PDP group
included weight loss, reduced waist circumference, improved diet
quality, and enhanced gut microbiome diversity, particularly among
participants with high adherence. However, the study had limitations:
LDL-C improvement was not statistically significant, the sample was
predominantly female (\\\~86%), limiting generalizability, and adherence
was self-reported, which may introduce bias (Bermingham KM, et al).

The study "Precision CardioDiet: Transforming Cardiac Care with
AI-Driven Dietary Recommendations" aimed to design a machine learning
(ML)-based personalized dietary recommendation system specifically for
cardiac patients. A classification-based approach using Neural Random
Forest and other ML models (Random Forest, SVM, Naïve Bayes, Decision
Tree) was developed to categorize foods as \"heart-healthy\" or \"not
heart-healthy.\" The proposed system demonstrated higher accuracy than
traditional models in delivering dietary guidance. It also explored the
synergistic effects of food combinations (e.g., proteins and
carbohydrates) on heart health. The research lacked clinical validation,
with no real-world application or patient outcome tracking. Cultural
dietary differences and long-term adherence were not considered (Moz,
S.H. et al).

The reviewed studies demonstrate diverse yet complementary approaches to
personalizing dietary interventions in cardiovascular care. Machine
learning models offer a promising framework for prediction and
classification but lack direct implementation in clinical dietary
guidance. Simulation-based and AI-generated menu studies suggest that
current AI tools can produce generalized recommendations, though they
often fall short in accuracy and specificity compared to structured
dietary models. Clinical and real-world interventions like those
involving traditional diets or biomarker-informed personalization show
measurable improvements in health outcomes, though often in short
durations and small cohorts. Personalization appears most effective when
incorporating multi-level data, including biomarkers, lifestyle, and
microbiota. However, challenges remain in adherence, validation,
cultural relevance, and scalability of these personalized systems.
Effective integration of technology with clinical dietary practice will
require iterative testing, user-centered design, and larger randomized
trials.

In cardiovascular disease management, AI-driven models exhibit strengths
in analyzing large datasets and classifying heart-healthy foods with
high accuracy, facilitating rapid and scalable personalization.
Nevertheless, their current clinical validation is lacking, with
AI-generated diet plans often showing limited adherence to established
dietary models such as DASH or Mediterranean diets, partly due to
generic or insufficiently tailored recommendations. Nutritionist-guided
approaches, including traditional dietary interventions and personalized
programs informed by biomarker profiling, demonstrate measurable
improvements in lipid profiles, blood pressure, and gut microbiome
diversity, linked to better patient engagement and cultural relevance.
While more labor-intensive and potentially costlier on a per-patient
basis, nutritionist-led care benefits from clinical expertise and
behavioral support fostering adherence. AI applications in CVD care
remain promising but need integration with human oversight to address
contextual nuances and promote sustained adherence, ensuring effective
translation into real-world practice.

**3.2.4 Personalized Nutrition in Chronic Kidney Disease**

Personalized Nutrition in Chronic Kidney Disease highlights the shift
from one-size-fits-all diets to individualized strategies in CKD. The
authors set out to summarize current CKD dietary guidelines, discuss
multi-omics in personalized nutrition, and address nutritional
challenges in CKD care. It is a narrative literature review drawing on
recent evidence and expert opinion. Pradhan et al. note that standard
CKD diets (protein, sodium, potassium restrictions) often lead to poor
adherence and outcomes. In contrast, personalized plans tailored to
genetics, phenotype, and preferences have shown promise across chronic
diseases and may benefit CKD patients. They conclude that despite
barriers (cost, data integration), personalized nutrition could improve
CKD outcomes and quality of life through targeted dietary strategies.
The review is largely conceptual; it notes a lack of CKD-specific trials
and "real-world" evidence. The authors call for further research on
implementing multi-omics and other personalized approaches in diverse
CKD populations (Pradhan N, Kerner J, Campos LA, Dobre M).

"AI-Powered Renal Diet Support: Performance of ChatGPT, Bard AI, and
Bing Chat"aimed to evaluate the performance and consistency of four
artificial intelligence (AI) language models---ChatGPT 3.5, ChatGPT 4,
Bard AI, and Bing Chat---in accurately classifying foods based on
potassium and phosphorus content, which is critical for managing chronic
kidney disease (CKD) diets. A total of 240 food items were selected from
the Mayo Clinic Renal Diet Handbook, with 149 items assessed for
potassium content and 91 for phosphorus. The AI models were prompted to
classify each item as either high or low in potassium or high in
phosphorus. The responses were then compared against Mayo Clinic
guidelines. Each AI model was tested on specific dates, and in the case
of ChatGPT 3.5, the process was repeated for consistency checks. The
study used descriptive statistics and agreement measures such as Cohen's
kappa to quantify accuracy and model consistency. ChatGPT 4 had the
highest accuracy (81%) in identifying potassium content, particularly
excelling in high-potassium food recognition (99% accuracy). Bard AI and
Bing Chat followed closely with 79% and 81% overall accuracy,
respectively. Bard AI uniquely achieved a perfect score (100%) for
phosphorus classification, while ChatGPT 3.5 performed moderately (66%
accuracy for potassium and 85% for phosphorus). Despite general success
in high-potassium and high-phosphorus food identification, accuracy
varied significantly in classifying low-potassium foods. The study also
revealed inconsistencies in responses across repeated sessions with the
same model, notably in ChatGPT 3.5. The study was limited by its use of
a fixed and controlled food dataset from a single source, which may not
represent the full variability of real-world food compositions.
Additionally, the AI models' underlying decision-making processes were
not examined in depth, and their binary classification approach lacked
contextual personalization. The models were not evaluated in clinical
practice settings, and aspects such as patient usability and clinical
integration were not addressed (Qarajeh A, et al)

Diet-Induced Proteomic and Metabolomic Signatures in CKD: A Precision
Nutrition Approach examines how diet-related molecular changes inform
CKD risk. The authors explore the "molecular signatures" of diet
(proteins and metabolites) associated with CKD development and
progression. It is a narrative review of studies using proteomics and
metabolomics in blood, urine, and kidney tissue samples to link dietary
components or patterns with CKD-related biomarkers. The review reports
that high-fat or Western diets alter expression of proteins involved in
energy metabolism, inflammation, and fibrosis in the kidney, and that
metabolic disturbances (e.g. in fatty acid, glucose, and amino acid
pathways) are key CKD indicators. It highlights metabolomic signatures
of plant protein intake and gut-microbiome--targeted diets (prebiotics)
that associate with reduced inflammation and toxin levels. Importantly,
integrating proteomic/metabolomic data could enable precision dietary
recommendations to mitigate CKD risk. The authors note that most
evidence is emerging (often in animal or small human studies) and call
for large-scale clinical trials to validate these molecular findings.
They stress that translating omics signatures into practice will require
further research before actionable personalized diets can be established
(Cabała S, Herosimczyk A).

Critical Review of Early Pharmacological and Dietary Support in CKD
covers early nephroprotection in moderate-to-severe CKD. The authors
review combined drug and diet therapies started early in CKD to slow
renal decline and improve well-being. It is a narrative review
summarizing current pharmacological treatments (e.g. ACE inhibitors,
SGLT2 inhibitors) and diet modifications. Danneel et al. emphasize that
combining drugs with salt and protein restriction synergistically
enhances kidney protection. They note that guideline-supported drug
therapies are more effective when paired with dietary measures (e.g.
reducing intraglomerular pressure via low sodium). The review also
advocates interdisciplinary care: including dietitians and pharmacists
alongside nephrologists could greatly improve patient education and
outcomes. The authors point out that few studies have formally evaluated
such interdisciplinary nutritional support; they call for future
research on team-based CKD management and on the nutritional components
(like protein targets) that best preserve kidney function. In summary,
this review reinforces the role of individualized dietary counseling
(salt/protein restriction) in early CKD treatment, noting that
collaborative care models remain under-tested (Danneel C, Sauvage C,
Hayef MN, Desmet V, Surquin M, Nortier J, et al).

The reviewed articles collectively underscore the growing momentum
toward personalized nutrition in chronic kidney disease (CKD),
highlighting both conceptual advances and practical tools. Personalized
Nutrition in Chronic Kidney Disease offers a visionary shift from
standardized dietary guidelines to patient-specific approaches guided by
genetic, phenotypic, and preference data. However, the lack of clinical
trials and real-world implementation data makes its conclusions largely
theoretical. In contrast, AI-Powered Renal Diet Support provides a
practical evaluation of AI tools (ChatGPT, Bard, Bing) in assessing food
nutrient content relevant to CKD management. While its quantitative
framework lends strength, its use of a fixed dataset and the absence of
clinical outcome validation limit its translational value. The article
Diet-Induced Proteomic and Metabolomic Signatures in CKD bridges the
conceptual and mechanistic domains, illustrating how diet affects
molecular pathways in CKD via proteomic and metabolomic signatures. It
compellingly supports precision nutrition through molecular biomarkers
but is hindered by reliance on preclinical or small-scale studies.
Together, these works reflect a progression from concept to mechanism to
tool, but also reveal gaps: a lack of integration between omics data, AI
decision support, and clinical application. Notably absent are
large-scale, randomized trials validating personalized dietary
strategies in CKD patients. Moving forward, integrating AI-driven tools
with omics-based personalization in clinical workflows---and validating
them in diverse populations---remains essential for translating this
promising paradigm into improved outcomes.

Personalized nutrition for chronic kidney disease (CKD) reflects a
complex interplay of clinical, multi-omics, and AI-driven tools.
AI-powered systems can accurately classify renal-relevant dietary
components, offering scalable decision support for managing potassium
and phosphorus intake critical in CKD. However, their reliance on fixed
datasets and binary classification limits flexibility and
patient-specific context, while lacking clinical outcome validation
restricts real-world application. Nutritionist-guided diets emphasize
individualized counseling integrating genotype, phenotype, and patient
preferences, potentially improving adherence and quality of life but
requiring significant healthcare resources and interdisciplinary
collaboration. The cost-effectiveness of either approach remains
underexplored, with challenges in implementing real-world trials to
validate long-term benefits. Future models combining AI\'s efficiency
with nutritionists\' clinical insight may enhance CKD dietary management
by optimizing scalability, personalization, and patient adherence in
routine care.

**3.2.5** **Critical Review of AI-Driven Personalized Nutrition in Gut
Microbiome:**

\"Accurate population proxies do not exist between 11.7 and 15 ka in
North America\" critically examines the validity of using
radiocarbon-dated event-count modeling as a proxy for human and
megafaunal population dynamics in North America during the late
Quaternary, specifically between 11.7 and 15 thousand years ago (ka).
The authors reanalyzed the archaeological and faunal radiocarbon
datasets used in Stewart et al. (2021), focusing on data integrity,
context, and taphonomic biases. They assessed the proportion of dates
derived from non-archaeological contexts and evaluated the impact of
taphonomic loss and sampling bias on faunal data. The study found that a
significant portion (25.8%) of the human population proxy dates were not
from archaeological contexts, leading to overestimation of human
presence and abundance. The faunal dataset was also shown to be heavily
influenced by taphonomic loss and biased sampling, particularly from a
few well-dated sites. The authors argue that these flaws render the
datasets unsuitable for accurate time-series correlation analyses and
question previous conclusions that climate, rather than humans, drove
megafaunal extinctions. While the critique is thorough, it is limited by
the inherent quality of available datasets and does not offer new
empirical data. The analysis is restricted to North America and may not
apply to regions with different taphonomic conditions (Pelton SR, Mackie
ME, Kelly R, Surovell TA).

\"Artificial intelligence--enabled microbiome-based precision nutrition
in patients with inflammatory bowel disease: a randomized controlled
trial\" aimed to evaluate the efficacy of an artificial intelligence
(AI)-driven, microbiome-based precision nutrition intervention in
patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Participants with IBD
were randomized to receive either an AI-personalized diet, designed
based on individual microbiome profiles and clinical data, or a
standard-of-care diet. Outcomes included changes in clinical disease
activity, quality of life, and gut microbiome composition, measured
before and after the intervention. The AI-personalized nutrition group
showed significant improvements in disease activity indices and quality
of life compared to the control group. Additionally, beneficial shifts
in gut microbiome diversity and composition were observed, suggesting
that personalized dietary interventions can modulate the microbiome and
improve clinical outcomes in IBD patients.\
The study\'s limitations include a relatively short intervention period
and a modest sample size, which may affect the generalizability of the
findings. Long-term adherence and sustainability of the dietary changes
were not assessed (Heithoff DM, Barnes LV, Mahan SP, Fried JC,
Fitzgibbons LN, House JK, et al).

"The Influence of an AI-Driven Personalized Nutrition Program on the
Human Gut Microbiome and Its Health Implications" aim of this study was
to evaluate the impact of a six-week intervention using an AI-based
personalized nutrition mobile application (the PROTEIN app) on the gut
microbiome composition of healthy adults. Secondary objectives included
assessing changes in dietary intake, anthropometric and biochemical
parameters, and exploring associations between dietary changes and
microbial genera abundance. Twenty-nine healthy adults (mean age 35
years, 52% female) participated in a six-week intervention during which
they used the PROTEIN mobile app. This app provided personalized meal
and activity plans generated by an AI advisor that tailored
recommendations based on individual profiles and Mediterranean diet
principles. Assessments included collection of fecal and blood samples
at baseline and after six weeks, with gut microbiome analysis performed
via 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing. Dietary intake was evaluated using a
validated food frequency questionnaire, while anthropometric and
biochemical parameters were also recorded. Throughout the intervention,
participants received initial goal-setting and ongoing support from a
nutritionist to enhance adherence and optimize outcomes. The study
observed significant increases in gut microbial diversity, with the
Chao1 richness index rising from 220.4 ± 58.5 to 241.5 ± 60.2 (p =
0.024) and Faith's phylogenetic diversity increasing from 15.5 ± 3.3 to
17.3 ± 2.8 (p = 0.0001). Changes in microbial composition included
increases in beneficial genera such as the Eubacterium coprostanoligenes
group and Oscillobacter, which are linked to reduced cholesterol and
cardiovascular risk, alongside decreases in inflammation-associated
genera like the Eubacterium ruminantium group and Gastranaerophilales.
Butyrate-producing genera showed mixed responses, with Faecalibacterium
increasing and Bifidobacterium decreasing. Dietary intake shifted with
significant reductions in carbohydrate (from 272.2 ± 97.7 to 222.9 ±
80.5 g, p = 0.003) and protein consumption (from 113.6 ± 38.8 to 98.6 ±
32.4 g, p = 0.011), while olive oil intake increased and correlated
positively with beneficial microbial changes. Anthropometric measures
showed a significant decrease in waist circumference (from 78.4 ± 12.1
to 77.2 ± 11.2 cm, p = 0.023). Biochemically, improvements were noted in
triglyceride levels, which correlated with alterations in specific
microbial taxa. The study had several limitations that should be
considered when interpreting the results. First, the small sample size
of only 29 healthy adults restricts the generalizability of the findings
to broader or clinical populations. The short duration of the
intervention, lasting only six weeks, means that the long-term effects
of the AI-driven personalized nutrition program remain unknown.
Additionally, dietary intake data were self-reported, which may
introduce recall bias and affect data accuracy. The absence of a
parallel control group limits the ability to conclusively attribute the
observed changes solely to the intervention. Finally, since the study
excluded individuals with chronic diseases, the results may not be
applicable to populations with metabolic or other health conditions
(Rouskas K, Guela M, Pantoura M, Pagkalos I, Hassapidou M, Lalama E, et
al).

The three reviewed studies present diverse applications of AI and data
modeling in understanding and influencing human health and population
trends, spanning both prehistoric demographic modeling and modern
personalized nutrition interventions. The study "Accurate population
proxies do not exist between 11.7 and 15 ka in North America" provides a
critical reassessment of radiocarbon-dated event-count modeling used to
infer population and megafaunal dynamics in late Quaternary North
America. It highlights significant methodological flaws, including the
inclusion of non-archaeological radiocarbon dates (25.8%) and the impact
of taphonomic and sampling biases. The critique undermines the
reliability of previous conclusions attributing megafaunal extinction
primarily to climate change rather than human activity. However, its
scope is limited by the quality of existing datasets and the absence of
new empirical data, and its conclusions are region-specific.In contrast,
the next three studies focus on AI-driven personalized nutrition and its
implications for gut microbiome and health. The "Artificial
intelligence--enabled microbiome-based precision nutrition in patients
with inflammatory bowel disease" RCT demonstrates that
microbiome-informed dietary interventions guided by AI can significantly
improve disease activity and quality of life in IBD patients. The study
shows the potential of AI to tailor therapeutic nutrition based on
individual gut microbiota profiles, but limitations such as small sample
size, short duration, and lack of long-term follow-up restrict its
applicability to broader clinical contexts. The study "The Influence of
an AI-Driven Personalized Nutrition Program on the Human Gut Microbiome
and Its Health Implications" explores the effects of a mobile AI-driven
dietary app (PROTEIN) on healthy adults over six weeks. It reports
positive changes in microbial diversity and composition, dietary intake,
and metabolic markers such as triglycerides and waist circumference.
However, it is constrained by a small sample size, self-reported dietary
data, short intervention period, and lack of a control group, limiting
causal inferences. Also, the study's focus on healthy individuals
excludes those with chronic health conditions, reducing its clinical
relevance. Together, these studies illustrate the promise and current
limitations of AI-based approaches in both historical population
modeling and contemporary personalized health interventions. The
demographic critique reveals how methodological flaws in data sourcing
can significantly skew interpretations of ancient population trends,
while the nutrition-focused studies highlight how individualized,
microbiome-based interventions may revolutionize chronic disease
management and health optimization. Nevertheless, across all studies,
challenges related to sample size, study duration, generalizability, and
long-term effectiveness must be addressed through larger, controlled,
and more diverse future trials.

**3.2.6 Comparative Analysis of AI-Driven and Nutritionist-Guided
Personalized Nutrition Approaches**

\"Effects of Intermittent Fasting on Health Markers in Humans \" aims to
systematically review the effects of various intermittent fasting (IF)
regimens on health markers in humans, focusing on metabolic,
cardiovascular, and inflammatory parameters. The authors conducted a
comprehensive literature search of clinical trials and observational
studies examining IF protocols, including alternate-day fasting,
time-restricted feeding, and periodic fasting. Data extraction and
quality assessment were performed using standard systematic review
guidelines. The review found that IF can lead to significant
improvements in body weight, insulin sensitivity, lipid profiles, and
blood pressure. Some studies also reported reductions in inflammatory
markers. However, the magnitude of benefits varied depending on the
fasting regimen and participant characteristics. The review notes
heterogeneity in study designs, fasting protocols, and outcome measures,
making direct comparisons challenging. The majority of included studies
were short-term, and long-term effects remain unclear (Kassem H, Beevi
A, Basheer S, Lutfi G, Ismail LC, Papandreou D).

"AI Nutrition Recommendation Using a Deep Generative Model and ChatGPT"
The objective of this study was to develop and validate a novel AI-based
nutrition recommendation system that combines a deep generative network
with the capabilities of ChatGPT. The goal was to generate highly
accurate, nutritious, and personalized weekly meal plans that align with
established nutritional guidelines, while leveraging the speed and
variety offered by large language models.The proposed system utilizes a
variational autoencoder (VAE) to model user-specific information (such
as anthropometric measurements and medical conditions) in a descriptive
latent space. Sophisticated loss functions are designed to align the
generated meal plans with nutritional guidelines from authorities like
the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and World Health Organization
(WHO). An optimizer adjusts meal quantities to meet users' energy
requirements. ChatGPT is integrated to expand the pool of meal options,
increasing variety and generalization. The system was evaluated using
3,000 virtual user profiles and 1,000 real user profiles, generating
over 91,000 daily meal plans. Its performance was compared to
traditional methods and assessed for accuracy, explainability, and
adherence to nutritional requirements. The AI-based system demonstrated
exceptional accuracy in generating weekly meal plans that met users'
energy and nutritional needs, outperforming traditional recommendation
systems in both virtual and real user scenarios. The integration of
ChatGPT enabled the system to provide a diverse range of culturally
varied meals, enhancing user satisfaction and generalizability. The
explainability of the generated plans and the alignment with
expert-validated nutritional rules were key strengths. The study also
showed that the proposed approach could be easily integrated into future
diet recommendation systems, offering both scalability and
personalization. A primary limitation is that, despite the improved
accuracy and diversity, the system's reliance on AI-generated meal
options (especially from ChatGPT) could introduce occasional
inaccuracies in calorie counts or nutrient composition, as noted in
prior research. The system was validated mainly on virtual and real user
profiles rather than in long-term, real-world clinical trials, so its
impact on actual health outcomes remains to be established.
Additionally, while the system aligns with established guidelines, it
does not yet fully incorporate dynamic feedback from nutrition
professionals or user adherence data, which could further enhance
personalization and safety (Papastratis I, Konstantinidis D, Daras P,
Dimitropoulos K).

"Digital disruption of dietetics: are we ready?" aims to outline how
digital health technologies are transforming the delivery of nutrition
care, specifically through the disruption of traditional dietetic
services. The review focuses on the integration of digital health tools
within the Nutrition Care Process (NCP), highlighting both opportunities
and challenges for dietitians in adopting these innovations. The authors
conducted a narrative review of recent literature and policy documents,
synthesizing findings related to digital health technologies---such as
electronic health records, mobile apps, wearables, artificial
intelligence, and telehealth---in the context of dietetic practice. The
review is structured according to the four steps of the NCP: assessment,
diagnosis, intervention, and monitoring/evaluation The review highlights
that digital tools such as mobile apps and wearables are increasingly
enhancing nutrition assessment by enabling real-time tracking of dietary
intake, body weight, and other health parameters. While these
technologies offer the potential to improve accuracy and efficiency in
assessment, concerns remain regarding the reliability of self-reported
data and the necessity for robust, evidence-based algorithms. In the
area of nutrition diagnosis, artificial intelligence and machine
learning are emerging as promising solutions, particularly for
automating risk prediction using electronic medical record (EMR) data.
However, the application of these technologies in practical dietetic
settings is still limited. Technology-assisted interventions---including
telehealth, webinars, and mobile health (mHealth) apps---have shown
effectiveness in facilitating behavior change and supporting chronic
disease management, though evidence about their long-term efficacy is
mixed. Additionally, digital health tools support remote monitoring and
the collection of patient-reported outcomes, which can enhance
self-efficacy and streamline the evaluation of interventions. The review
also provides an overview of frameworks and resources such as the NHS
Apps Library, ORCHA, and Nutritools that assist practitioners in
selecting and implementing appropriate digital tools in their
practice.Despite these advancements, the review acknowledges several
limitations. It is a narrative review rather than a systematic one,
which introduces the possibility of selection bias. The rapid pace of
technological change means that some findings may quickly become
outdated. Furthermore, the evidence base is largely drawn from studies
conducted in high-income countries, potentially limiting the
generalizability of the results to other contexts. There is also a
limited discussion regarding the effectiveness of specific digital
technologies in diverse populations, and the review notes a lack of
robust clinical trial data to support many digital interventions. These
limitations underscore the need for ongoing research and critical
evaluation as digital health continues to (Kelly JT, Collins PF,
McCamley J, Ball L, Roberts S, Campbell KL)

The article \"Personalized Nutrition and Multiomics Analyses: A Guide
for Nutritionists\" by Leila M. Shinn and Hannah D. Holscher (2021) aims
to provide nutrition professionals with a foundational understanding of
personalized nutrition, particularly focusing on the integration of
multiomics analyses and machine learning tools. The review discusses how
these advanced scientific methods can be leveraged to tailor dietary
recommendations to individuals based on their unique physiological and
genetic makeup.This is a narrative review that synthesizes current
literature and expert opinion on the concepts of personalized nutrition,
multiomics (including genomics, metabolomics, proteomics, and microbiome
analysis), and machine learning. The article also reviews research on
the development and use of objective biomarkers for food intake and
health status, providing practical examples and definitions to guide
nutritionists in evaluating studies and future applications.The review
highlights that personalized nutrition is an emerging field that seeks
to optimize dietary recommendations by considering individual
differences in physiology, genetics, microbiome composition, and
lifestyle. Multiomics approaches allow for comprehensive molecular
profiling, offering insights into how diet interacts with biological
systems. Machine learning and artificial intelligence are increasingly
used to analyze large, complex datasets, enabling the identification of
patterns and the prediction of health outcomes. The article provides
examples of how supervised and unsupervised machine learning models can
be applied in nutrition research and practice. Importantly, the review
underscores the need for objective biomarkers to accurately assess
dietary intake and nutritional status, as traditional self-report
methods have significant limitations. The authors emphasize the
importance of interdisciplinary collaboration and the central role of
registered dietitian nutritionists in translating scientific advances
into personalized dietary guidance.The article is a narrative review and
does not employ a systematic methodology, which may introduce selection
bias. The field of personalized nutrition is rapidly evolving, and some
discussed technologies and findings may quickly become outdated. There
is also a lack of consensus on definitions and standards for
personalized nutrition, and the review notes ongoing challenges in
developing universally accepted biomarkers and objective dietary
assessment tools. Additionally, much of the current research is based on
populations from high-income countries, which may limit the
applicability of findings to diverse groups (Shinn LM, Holscher HD).

\"Nutritional Strategies for Managing Inflammation in Chronic Disease\"
reviews nutritional strategies for managing inflammation in chronic
diseases such as obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease . The
authors reviewed RCTs and cohort studies on anti-inflammatory diets,
specific nutrients (omega-3 fatty acids, antioxidants), and dietary
patterns. Anti-inflammatory diets, such as the Mediterranean and DASH
diets, are associated with lower levels of inflammatory markers (CRP,
IL-6). Omega-3 supplementation and increased intake of fruits and
vegetables also show benefits. The review notes that individual
responses to dietary interventions vary, and more research is needed to
personalize anti-inflammatory nutrition strategies (Agrawal K, Goktas P,
Holtkemper M, Beecks C, Kumar N).

\"The Role of Dietary Patterns in Mental Health: Emerging Evidence\"
examines the relationship between dietary patterns and mental health
outcomes, including depression and anxiety .The authors synthesized data
from observational studies, RCTs, and meta-analyses on the effects of
Mediterranean, Western, and other dietary patterns on mental health.
Adherence to healthy dietary patterns, particularly the Mediterranean
diet, is associated with lower risk of depression and anxiety. Diets
high in processed foods and sugars are linked to poorer mental health
outcomes. Most evidence is observational, and causality cannot be
established. There is a need for more RCTs and exploration of underlying
mechanisms (Kim DW, Park JS, Sharma K, Velazquez A, Li L, Ostrominski
JW, et al).

Collectively, these six papers chart a promising---but still
fragmented---path toward precision nutrition and lifestyle medicine. The
rigorous systematic review of intermittent fasting provides the
strongest empirical anchor, yet its short‐term trials and protocol
heterogeneity mirror a wider evidence gap echoed in the narrative
reviews. Both the AI‑driven meal‑planning study and the "digital
disruption" overview showcase how machine learning, wearables and
telehealth can scale personalization and engagement, but they also
expose new vulnerabilities: algorithmic calorie‑count errors, opaque
models, and a paucity of long‑term, clinician‑mediated outcomes. Shinn
and Holscher's primer on multiomics usefully demystifies emerging
biomarker and ML workflows, yet underscores the absence of consensus
standards and the high‑income bias that likewise limits the inflammation
and mental‑health diet reviews. Across papers, benefits repeatedly
cluster around Mediterranean‑style patterns, anti‑inflammatory nutrient
profiles, and technology‑enhanced behavioral monitoring; however,
cross‑study comparability suffers from inconsistent endpoints,
self‑report bias, and limited diversity in study populations. Future
work must integrate multiomics, real‑world adherence data, and longer
RCTs within interoperable digital ecosystems to determine whether these
innovative tools translate into sustained metabolic, cardiometabolic,
and mental‑health gains across varied socio‑cultural contexts.

**3.2.7 Critical Review of Personalized Nutrition for COVID-19:**

Nutrition  as Personalized Medicine against SARS‑CoV‑2 Infections
surveyed clinical, molecular‑immunological and metabolomic data to
propose diet‑tailored recommendations---ranging from ketogenic and
Mediterranean patterns to targeted fatty‑acid modulation---for
vulnerable groups (pregnant women, cancer patients) during acute
COVID‑19. The authors link specific macronutrient profiles (e.g., high
n‑3 PUFA, low n‑6 PUFA) and micronutrient adequacy to dampening
cytokine‑storm pathways, supporting epithelial barrier integrity and
regulating ACE‑2 expression. Evidence is largely extrapolated from small
clinical studies or pre‑clinical work; no unified criteria were used to
grade study quality, and personalization frameworks remain theoretical
(Dellino M, Cascardi E, Vinciguerra M, Lamanna B, Malvasi A, Scacco S,
et al).

Precision Nutrition to Reset Virus‑Induced Human Metabolic Reprogramming
and Dysregulation (HMRD) in Long‑COVID maps how SARS‑CoV‑2 hijacks host
metabolism and discusses nutrient "toolkits" (e.g., N‑acetyl‑cysteine,
nicotinamide riboside, specialized pro‑resolving mediators) to restore
metabolic homeostasis in post‑acute sequelae. Precision combinations of
antioxidants, anti‑inflammatories and microbiota‑directed substrates are
proposed to target four phenotypic clusters of long‑COVID, with
preliminary trials showing improvements in fatigue scores and redox
biomarkers. Heterogeneity across RCT designs, small sample sizes, and
lack of standardized outcome measures limit generalizability;
mechanistic links are mostly inferred rather than confirmed in large
cohorts (Naidu AS, Wang CK, Rao P, Mancini F, Clemens RA,
Wirakartakusumah A, et al).

"Evaluation of Country Dietary Habits Using Machine Learning Techniques
in Relation to Deaths from COVID-19"aimed to analyze the relationship
between dietary habits across 170 countries and COVID-19-related
mortality rates, using machine learning to uncover dietary patterns that
correlate with higher death rates. The goal was to identify risk-prone
nutritional behaviors that could inform preventive strategies during
pandemics. The researchers utilized the COVID-19 Healthy Diet Dataset,
which includes data on food consumption (in kilograms, kilocalories,
fat, and protein content) across 23 food categories. Additional data on
obesity, undernourishment, and COVID-19 cases/deaths were included.
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to reduce 94 nutritional
variables to 23 principal components retaining 95% data variance. Then,
K-Means clustering was used to group countries into 20 clusters based on
their dietary profiles. The Davies--Bouldin Index helped determine the
optimal number of clusters. Clusters were evaluated for mortality
distribution using the 3rd quartile of death percentages for realism,
avoiding outlier influence. Three clusters (clusters 3, 4, and 17),
encompassing 30 countries, were identified as high-risk in terms of
COVID-19 mortality. These countries shared common dietary
characteristics: high intake of animal products, milk, meat,
sugars/sweeteners, and animal fats, along with low consumption of
cereals. They also had higher average caloric consumption (3277.5
Kcal/day) compared to low-risk countries (2764.3 Kcal/day), and a
significantly higher prevalence of obesity. Interestingly,
undernourishment levels were lower in high-risk countries. Notably, 15
of these 30 countries were also among the top 30 in COVID-19 deaths
globally as of May 2020, including the USA, Spain, Italy, and France.
The study did not account for non-dietary risk factors such as race, age
distribution, healthcare infrastructure, government response policies,
or population density. The clustering method grouped countries only by
nutritional data, which, although insightful, could miss other complex
socio-environmental interactions. There was no analysis of dietary
quality beyond broad food group intake; micronutrient or dietary
diversity considerations were absent (García-Ordás MT, et al).

### "Personalized nutrition: perspectives on challenges, opportunities, and guiding principles for data use and fusion" sets out to map the data‑related bottlenecks and enablers that stand between today's siloed nutrition datasets and tomorrow's fully integrated, equitable personalised‑nutrition (PN) ecosystems. Authored by a cross‑sector panel, it synthesises literature and the proceedings of the 21 Aug 2023 Illinois "Personalized Nutrition Data: Challenges & Opportunities" workshop, which grouped ten invited talks into three data domains---health & biological; social, behavioural & environmental; and consumer‑purchasing data---followed by breakout discussions; the narrative review that follows is structured around those domains rather than a systematic protocol. Key results are threefold. (1) Domain‑specific insights: the paper catalogues exemplar datasets (e.g., All of Us, PREDICT, retail‑scanner panels), showcases successful multi‑omics and wearable‑sensor use cases, and dissects behavioural taxonomies such as the Human Behaviour Change Project. (2) Cross‑cutting challenges: data silos, lack of standard nomenclature, bias toward high‑income cohorts, privacy hurdles, and scarce long‑term outcomes data are repeatedly highlighted. (3) Action agenda: five guiding principles---build harmonised data architecture, adopt systems biology, incentivise sharing, safeguard individual control, and focus on accessible, actionable outputs---are proposed as a road map for researchers, industry and regulators. The authors acknowledge that their narrative, workshop‑anchored approach risks selection bias and may date rapidly in a fast‑moving field; moreover, many cited exemplars remain proof‑of‑concept rather than long‑term, real‑world evaluations, and representation of underserved groups is still limited (Donovan SM, Abrahams M, Anthony JC, Bao Y, Barragan M, Bermingham KM, et al).

The four articles collectively highlight the growing importance and
complexity of personalized nutrition in the context of COVID-19 and
beyond, yet they also reveal significant gaps that limit current
translational impact. The review on nutritional strategies against
SARS-CoV-2 infection emphasizes promising molecular and immunomodulatory
pathways---such as modulation of fatty acid profiles and micronutrient
sufficiency---to mitigate disease severity, especially in vulnerable
populations; however, its heavy reliance on small-scale or preclinical
data and lack of unified quality assessment undermine the robustness of
personalized dietary recommendations. Similarly, the precision nutrition
approach to long-COVID elucidates relevant metabolic derailments and
proposes targeted nutrient "toolkits" to restore homeostasis, with
preliminary clinical signals of efficacy. However, diverse study
designs, small cohorts, and unstandardized outcomes weaken
generalizability, and mechanistic claims largely remain speculative
without large-scale validation. The machine learning analysis relating
country-level dietary patterns to COVID-19 mortality adds a valuable
epidemiological dimension, identifying dietary clusters associated with
heightened risk driven by high animal product and sugar consumption;
yet, the absence of crucial confounding factors---such as demographics,
healthcare quality, and policy responses---and the simplistic focus on
macronutrient groupings limit causal inference and nuance. Lastly, the
narrative review on personalized nutrition data infrastructures reveals
critical systemic challenges including data silos, limited diversity,
privacy concerns, and a scarcity of longitudinal outcomes, while
proposing a pragmatic roadmap emphasizing harmonization, systems
biology, and equitable access. However, its non-systematic methodology
and reliance on nascent proof-of-concept exemplars indicate that these
guiding principles require rigorous real-world testing and broader
inclusion of underserved populations. Together, these articles
underscore both the transformative potential and formidable challenges
facing personalized nutrition research, emphasizing the need for
integrated, large-scale, and multidisciplinary efforts to bridge
mechanistic insights, clinical utility, and data infrastructure toward
equitable and effective nutritional precision medicine.

**3.4 Cross-Domain Analysis**

1.  **Accuracy versus Individualization Balance**

Artificial intelligence achieves exactness through computational
capabilities but faces challenges converting data into customized
behavioral guidance; dietary specialists offer relevant context while
potentially lacking comprehensive analytical capabilities.

2.  **Reach and Availability Considerations**

AI platforms enable worldwide access and cost-effectiveness yet create
disparity risks when training information overlooks underserved
communities. Professional nutritionist services remain
resource-demanding but essential for high-risk cases.

3.  **Cultural and Moral Considerations**

Each approach must navigate cultural dietary variations, computational
prejudices, and information management. Upcoming models should
incorporate equity, inclusion, and openness as fundamental design
elements.

4.  **Combination Approaches**

Research indicates that merging AI data analysis with professional
supervision produces optimal balanced results---artificial intelligence
for assessment and tracking, human specialists for treatment and
guidance.

**3.5 Theoretical Framework for Partnership**

The combined research supports a \"Cooperative Intelligence\" approach,
where:

- AI platforms analyze and evaluate extensive data collections to
  determine ideal nutritional strategies.

- Dietary professionals confirm, contextualize, and personalize
  suggestions.

- Individuals participate via electronic response systems (applications,
  remote nutrition services).

This partnership might establish a dynamic learning environment where
ongoing feedback improves both AI algorithms and professional practices,
advancing individualized nutrition toward integrated precision medicine.

**4. Study Limitations and Findings:**

**4.1 Language and Publication Restrictions**

The inclusion criteria limited studies to English-language publications
only. This linguistic constraint potentially omitted valuable research
from non-English regions, particularly Asia, Latin America, and the
Middle East, where artificial intelligence developments and culturally
unique nutritional practices are advancing swiftly.

Additionally, publication bias may exist, given that research
demonstrating favorable or innovative AI results has greater likelihood
of appearing in indexed publications, which could inflate the perceived
efficacy of AI-driven interventions compared to nutrition
professional-led methods.

**4.2 Time Constraints and Study Timeline**

While the review timeframe encompassed studies from 2017 through 2024,
most artificial intelligence nutrition research has emerged only
recently. This brief publication period restricts our understanding of
durability, sustained compliance, and practical implementation outcomes.
Given the rapid advancement of AI technology, emerging models may
surpass or contradict previous results, indicating that regular review
updates will be essential for maintaining current relevance.

**4.3 Diverse Research Approaches**

Significant variation in research methodologies, information sources,
and measured outcomes precluded comprehensive meta-analytical synthesis.
Research approaches ranged from self-reported nutritional information to
machine learning algorithms and microbiome analysis, creating obstacles
for quantitative combination. Therefore, findings are presented through
narrative comparison, which provides conceptual depth but introduces
inherent interpretive bias.

**4.4 Absence of Uniform Assessment Standards**

The lack of consistent performance indicators for AI-driven nutrition
platforms complicates inter-study comparisons. Parameters including
precision, compliance rates, and user satisfaction lacked standardized
definitions, reflecting the interdisciplinary and developing
characteristics of this domain.

Likewise, nutrition professional-delivered interventions demonstrated
variation in frequency, length, and implementation methods, making
direct comparison across research datasets difficult.

**4.5 Insufficient Community-Based and Cross-Cultural Testing**

Most AI studies occurred within controlled research environments or
simulated digital settings rather than actual clinical or community
practice. Performance in real-world applications, particularly across
diverse cultural contexts and resource-limited environments, remains
inadequately examined.

Professional nutritionist interventions, while demonstrating greater
cultural responsiveness, also lacked consistent assessment across
different populations. The minimal representation of developing nations
highlights the necessity for culturally inclusive and equitable research
frameworks going forward.

**4.6 Privacy Protection and Moral Implications**

Limited AI research explicitly examined ethical, regulatory, and privacy
concerns related to utilizing sensitive health and genetic information.
As individualized nutrition increasingly depends on extensive data
integration, ethical oversight and responsible data management
frameworks will become essential for appropriate implementation.

**Conclusion**

This comprehensive systematic review explores how Artificial
Intelligence (AI) and nutritionist-led systems are transforming the
developing field of individualized nutrition. Through analyzing 30
studies spanning various diseases---such as diabetes, heart-related
conditions, chronic kidney disease, and gut microbiome disorders---a
distinct trend emerges: AI and human knowledge provide complementary
advantages that can substantially improve clinical nutrition results
when integrated.

AI-powered approaches show exceptional abilities in analyzing
multi-omics, behavioral, and clinical information to provide precision
dietary guidance. These systems facilitate effective large-scale data
processing, forecasting analytics, and immediate feedback systems that
prove challenging to accomplish through manual methods. Nevertheless,
these technologies frequently face difficulties with situational
customization, cultural flexibility, and behavioral
encouragement---domains where human nutritionists demonstrate
superiority.

Conversely, nutritionist-led programs---founded on understanding,
cultural awareness, and continuous guidance---regularly accomplish
greater adherence rates and patient satisfaction levels, although they
face constraints regarding scalability, personal bias, and accessibility
challenges in resource-constrained environments.

The research indicates the necessity for an integrated AI-nutritionist
approach that combines computational accuracy with human insight. Within
this unified structure, AI would handle sophisticated data processing,
risk forecasting, and recommendation development, while nutritionists
would interpret these findings, ensure ethical and culturally
appropriate implementation, and maintain patient involvement through
counseling sessions. This collaboration establishes a foundation for
shared intelligence in dietary healthcare.

Practical deployment of these systems requires thorough long-term
validation research to assess durability, safety, and clinical
effectiveness across varied populations. Equally crucial are ethical and
regulatory structures to protect data privacy, transparency, and
algorithmic equity---especially as AI technologies increasingly depend
on genetic and behavioral information. Cross-disciplinary cooperation
among data scientists, healthcare providers, dietitians, and
policymakers proves vital for developing strong, fair, and
patient-focused nutrition systems.

To conclude, the future of individualized nutrition does not involve
substituting human knowledge with algorithms, but rather integrating
AI\'s analytical capabilities with the empathy and situational awareness
of nutrition experts. Through developing such integrated, multicultural,
and research-based approaches, personalized nutrition can advance into a
scalable, ethically sound, and clinically successful element of
precision healthcare.
